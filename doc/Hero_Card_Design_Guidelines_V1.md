# Hero Card Design Guidelines V1

## Purpose

This document defines the opening Hero Card for Linear Algebra Bridge knowledge pages.

The Hero Card is a compact mathematical explanation. It is not a decorative illustration and it is not required to centre on a formula.

## 1. Preserve the Product Family

Retain the established Calculus Bridge qualities:

- white or very light card surface;
- thin cool-grey border;
- subtle shadow;
- restrained corner radius;
- deep navy mathematical content;
- brick red for a second object, contrast, or result;
- warm gold only as a limited third accent;
- small uppercase English eyebrow label;
- generous whitespace;
- precise mathematical typography.

Do not redesign the global brand for each topic.

## 2. Choose the Mathematical Centre

The visual centre must be the topic's most important object or relationship.

Possible centres:

- a system and its augmented matrix;
- an elementary row operation;
- a matrix-vector product;
- vectors combining to form another vector;
- a span or subspace relationship;
- a basis and a coordinate vector;
- a transformation before-and-after view;
- a determinant as signed scaling;
- an eigenvector whose direction is preserved;
- an orthogonal decomposition;
- a quadratic form and its principal axes.

A formula is appropriate only when it communicates the concept better than the object itself.

## 3. Concept Before Decoration

Before designing the card, write one sentence:

> The student should see that...

Every card element must support that sentence.

Remove:

- decorative grids with no coordinate meaning;
- arrows that do not represent a mapping, operation, or dependency;
- floating symbols unrelated to the displayed equation;
- generic matrices used only as a pattern;
- colour variation without semantic meaning;
- miniature labels that cannot be read on mobile.

## 4. Topic-Specific Hero Patterns

### Systems and Gaussian Elimination

Show an equation system and its augmented matrix, with the same coefficients linked by consistent colour or alignment. If showing an operation, name it and preserve solution-set equivalence.

### Matrix Operations

Show dimensions and the operation's structural rule. For multiplication, a row-column pairing or transformed vector is preferable to an unexplained result matrix.

### Invertibility

Show a reversible mapping or the relationship `AA^{-1}=I`. Do not imply that every nonzero matrix is invertible.

### Determinant

Show signed area/volume scaling only when the coordinate system and transformation are accurate. Distinguish magnitude from orientation.

### Linear Combination and Span

Show scaled vectors combining into a target and indicate the generated set. Do not use a single 2D picture to claim a theorem in arbitrary dimension.

### Linear Independence

Show the uniqueness or non-existence of a nontrivial zero combination. Avoid representing independence merely as vectors pointing in different-looking directions.

### Basis and Coordinates

Show one vector with two coordinate descriptions under two named bases. Keep the geometric vector distinct from its coordinate column.

### Rank, Kernel, and Image

Show mappings and dimension relationships. Identify domain, kernel, image, and codomain explicitly.

### Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Show a transformation that preserves the eigenvector's direction while scaling or reversing it. State the eigenvalue. Never include the zero vector as an eigenvector.

### Orthogonality and Projection

Show the right-angle relation, decomposition, and projection target. Equal-looking angles are not proof; include the inner-product relationship when relevant.

### Quadratic Forms

Show level sets or principal directions only when they correspond to the displayed symmetric matrix and sign structure.

## 5. Colour Semantics

Assign colour meanings before implementation.

Recommended roles:

- navy: primary/original object;
- brick red: transformed, compared, or selected object;
- gold: invariant, key scalar, or limited emphasis;
- pale blue-grey: space, container, or support region;
- neutral grey: secondary notation and construction lines.

Colour meaning must remain consistent inside the card and continue into the nearby explanation.

Do not rely on colour alone. Use labels, shape, position, or line style as a second cue.

## 6. Formula and Matrix Integrity

- Use real mathematical layout, not plain text approximations.
- Keep matrix brackets complete and vertically aligned.
- Keep rows and columns aligned.
- Show dimensions when compatibility matters.
- Do not split one matrix across unrelated responsive lines.
- Preserve superscripts, subscripts, transposes, inverses, and vector styling.
- Avoid slash notation when a structured fraction is the concept.
- Use scalable rendering at all supported widths.

## 7. Labels

- Keep labels short and bilingual only when both languages add value.
- Place labels close to their mathematical object without covering it.
- Use leader lines only when proximity is ambiguous.
- Maintain readable contrast over grids and shaded regions.
- Explain every legend item.
- Avoid an unlabeled `before → after` relationship.

## 8. Responsive Behaviour

Desktop may place text and Hero Card side by side.

On narrow screens:

- stack the card below the title and definitions;
- keep the mathematical object large enough to read;
- simplify secondary labels before shrinking essential notation;
- allow safe horizontal scrolling only for a genuinely wide mathematical object;
- never crop brackets, arrowheads, or labels;
- preserve aspect ratio for coordinate systems.

## 9. Hero Card Review Questions

- Can the student name the central object?
- Does the card express the page's core relationship?
- Are dimensions and coordinate conventions visible when necessary?
- Does each arrow represent a real operation or mapping?
- Are colours mathematically assigned?
- Does the card remain truthful without animation?
- Is the card readable at mobile width?
- Would removing any element make the mathematical idea less clear? If not, remove it.

## 10. Completion Rule

A visually attractive card fails if its mathematical meaning is vague. A mathematically correct card also fails if the student cannot identify its objects and relationships quickly.

Approval requires both mathematical integrity and immediate visual legibility.
